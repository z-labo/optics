Coupled-Wave Equations#

Source note. This notebook is from the course Nonlinear Optics, taught by Prof. G. G. Paulus at Friedrich Schiller University Jena in the winter semester 2021/22.

The derivation order, notation, and equation numbering follow the supplied lecture notes. Mathematical expressions have only been reformatted into notebook-friendly LaTeX.

Wave Equation for Nonlinear Optical Media#

From Maxwell’s equations to the general wave equation#

Derive the wave equation by inserting Ampère’s law into Faraday’s law:

\[ \vec{\nabla}\times\vec{\nabla}\times\widetilde{\vec E} = -\mu_0\partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec D} \]
\[ \widetilde{\vec D} = \varepsilon_0\widetilde{\vec E} + \widetilde{\vec P}, \qquad \mu_0=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2} \]
\[ \vec{\nabla}\times\vec{\nabla}\times\widetilde{\vec E} + \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec E} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2}\partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec P} \tag{5} \]

Equation (5) is the most general form of the wave equation used here in classical nonlinear optics.

Plane-wave condition and separation of polarization#

In optics, we would like to replace \(\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{\nabla}\times\widetilde{\vec E}\) with a Laplacian. In general, however, the divergence of the electric field is not zero. For a plane wave we use

\[ \nabla\cdot\vec E=0. \]

This leads to

\[ -\nabla^2\widetilde{\vec E} + \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec E} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2}\partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec P} \tag{6} \]
\[ \widetilde{\vec P} = \widetilde{\vec P}^{(1)} + \widetilde{\vec P}^{NL} \tag{7} \]
\[ -\nabla^2\widetilde{\vec E} + \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2 \left( \widetilde{\vec E} + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\widetilde{\vec P}^{(1)} \right) = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2} \partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec P}^{NL} \tag{8} \]

Using

\[ \widetilde{\vec D}^{(1)} = \varepsilon_0\widetilde{\vec E} + \widetilde{\vec P}^{(1)}, \]

we obtain

\[ -\nabla^2\widetilde{\vec E} + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2} \partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec D}^{(1)} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2} \partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec P}^{NL}. \tag{9} \]

Simple case: lossless, dispersionless medium#

For a lossless, dispersionless medium, assume an instantaneous linear response:

\[ \widetilde{\vec D}^{(1)}(t) = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon\, \widetilde{\vec E}(t) \tag{10} \]

Substituting Eq. (10) into Eq. (9),

\[ -\nabla^2\widetilde{\vec E} + \frac{\varepsilon^{(1)}}{c^2} \partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec E} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2} \partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec P}^{NL}. \tag{11} \]

In general, $\( D(t)\neq E(t), \qquad D(\omega)\sim E(\omega). \)$

Equation (11) is a wave equation with a source term: the nonlinear-polarization term on its right-hand side.

General case: dispersive media and monochromatic components#

For a dispersive medium, Eq. (10) does not hold in the time domain. In frequency space, however, the linear displacement and electric field are related by the frequency-dependent dielectric response.

A second-order nonlinear process couples three waves, while a third-order process couples four waves. Since only a few waves are involved, the total field can be written as a sum of monochromatic components.

\[ \widetilde{\vec D}^{(1)}(\omega) = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon^{(1)}(\omega) \widetilde{\vec E}(\omega) \]
\[ \widetilde{\vec E}(\vec r,t) = \sum_n' \widetilde{\vec E}_n(\vec r,t) \]
\[ \widetilde{\vec D}(\vec r,t) = \sum_n' \widetilde{\vec D}_n(\vec r,t) \]
\[ \widetilde{\vec P}^{NL}(\vec r,t) = \sum_n' \widetilde{\vec P}^{NL}_n(\vec r,t) \tag{13} \]

Assuming \(\varepsilon^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}\) and substituting the frequency component into Eq. (9),

\[ -\nabla^2\widetilde{\vec E}_n + \frac{\varepsilon^{(1)}(\omega_n)}{c^2} \partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec E}_n = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2} \partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec P}^{NL}_n. \tag{15} \]

For monochromatic waves,

\[ \widetilde{\vec E}_n(\vec r,t) = \vec E_n(\vec r)e^{-i\omega_nt} +c.c., \qquad \vec E_n\in\mathbb C^3, \]
\[ \widetilde{\vec D}_n(\vec r,t) = \vec D_n(\vec r)e^{-i\omega_nt} +c.c., \qquad \vec D_n\in\mathbb C^3, \]
\[ \widetilde{\vec P}_n(\vec r,t) = \vec P_n(\vec r)e^{-i\omega_nt} +c.c., \qquad \vec P_n\in\mathbb C^3. \tag{16} \]

Because the spatial and temporal dependences have been factorized,

\[ \partial_t^2\sim-\omega^2, \]

and therefore

\[ \nabla^2\widetilde{\vec E}_n(\vec r) + \frac{\varepsilon^{(1)}(\omega_n)\omega_n^2}{c^2} \widetilde{\vec E}_n = -\frac{\omega_n^2}{\varepsilon_0c^2} \widetilde{\vec P}^{NL}_n. \tag{17} \]

Equation (15) has the same algebraic form as Eq. (11), while allowing the material response to depend on frequency.

Coupled-Wave Equations for Sum-Frequency Generation (SFG)#

Field equation and slowly changing amplitude ansatz#

We now apply the preceding wave equation to a specific nonlinear interaction: sum-frequency generation (SFG).

For SFG,

\[ \omega_3=\omega_1+\omega_2. \]

Equation (15) for the \(\omega_3\) component becomes

\[ -\nabla^2\widetilde{\vec E}_3 + \frac{n_3^2}{c^2} \partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec E}_3 = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2} \partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec P}^{NL}_3. \tag{20} \]

If the right-hand side is negligible, the ordinary wave-equation solution is

\[ \widetilde E_3(z,t) = A_3e^{i(k_3z-\omega_3t)} +c.c., \]
\[ k_3=\frac{n_3\omega_3}{c}. \tag{21} \]

When the nonlinear source is small but nonzero, we use the ansatz

\[ \widetilde E_3(z,t) = A_3(z)e^{i(k_3z-\omega_3t)} +c.c. \]

The \(z\)-dependent amplitude is physically natural because the generated wave is expected to grow as it propagates through the crystal.

Nonlinear source polarization#

For the nonlinear source,

\[ \widetilde P_3(z,t) = P_3(z)e^{-i\omega_3t} +c.c., \]
\[ P_3 = 4\varepsilon_0d_{\mathrm{eff}}E_1E_2, \qquad 2d_{\mathrm{eff}}\sim\chi^{(2)}. \tag{24} \]

Also,

\[ E_i(z,t) = E_ie^{-i\omega_it}+c.c., \qquad E_i=A_ie^{ik_iz}. \tag{25} \]

Thus,

\[ P_3 = 4\varepsilon_0d_{\mathrm{eff}} A_1A_2e^{i(k_1+k_2)z}. \tag{26} \]

Substitute into the wave equation#

Substitute the field ansatz and Eq. (26) into Eq. (20).

First term:

\[ -\nabla^2\widetilde{\vec E}_3 = -\partial_z^2 \left[ A_3(z)e^{i(k_3z-\omega_3t)}+c.c. \right] \]
\[ = - \left[ \frac{\partial^2A_3}{\partial z^2} + 2ik_3\frac{\partial A_3}{\partial z} - k_3^2A_3 \right] e^{i(k_3z-\omega_3t)} +c.c. \]

Second term:

\[ \frac{n_3^2}{c^2}\partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec E}_3 = -\frac{n_3^2\omega_3^2}{c^2} \left[ A_3e^{i(k_3z-\omega_3t)}+c.c. \right]. \]

Third term:

\[ -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2} \partial_t^2\widetilde{\vec P}^{NL}_3 = \frac{4\varepsilon_0d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_3^2} {\varepsilon_0c^2} A_1A_2 e^{i[(k_1+k_2)z-\omega_3t]} +c.c. \]

Collect the terms#

Combining the terms,

\[ \left[ -\frac{\partial^2A_3}{\partial z^2} -2ik_3\frac{\partial A_3}{\partial z} +k_3^2A_3 -\frac{n_3^2\omega_3^2}{c^2}A_3 \right] e^{i(k_3z-\omega_3t)} +c.c. = \frac{4\varepsilon_0d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_3^2} {\varepsilon_0c^2} A_1A_2 e^{i[(k_1+k_2)z-\omega_3t]} +c.c. \]

Since

\[ k_3^2A_3-\frac{n_3^2\omega_3^2}{c^2}A_3=0, \]

and because \(e^{-i\omega t}\) and \(e^{i\omega t}\) are linearly independent, the \(c.c.\) term can be treated separately. Dividing by \(e^{-i\omega_3t}\) gives

\[ - \left[ \frac{\partial^2A_3}{\partial z^2} + 2ik_3\frac{\partial A_3}{\partial z} \right] e^{ik_3z} = \frac{4\varepsilon_0d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_3^2} {\varepsilon_0c^2} A_1A_2e^{i(k_1+k_2)z}. \tag{27} \]

Slowly varying amplitude approximation (SVAA)#

Under the slowly varying amplitude approximation (SVAA), the second derivative is neglected:

\[ \frac{\partial^2A_3}{\partial z^2} \ll k_3\frac{\partial A_3}{\partial z}. \]

Then

\[ -2ik_3\frac{\partial A_3}{\partial z}e^{ik_3z} = \frac{4\varepsilon_0d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_3^2} {\varepsilon_0c^2} A_1A_2e^{i(k_1+k_2)z}, \]

so that

\[ \frac{\partial A_3}{\partial z} = i\frac{2d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_3^2}{k_3c^2} A_1A_2e^{i(k_1+k_2-k_3)z}. \]

Define

\[ \Delta k=k_1+k_2-k_3, \qquad k_3=\frac{n_3\omega_3}{c}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{\partial A_3}{\partial z} = i\frac{2d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_3}{n_3c} A_1A_2e^{i\Delta kz}. \tag{28} \]

Coupled equations for all three waves#

The same procedure is applied to \(A_1\) and \(A_2\), using

\[ \omega_1=\omega_3-\omega_2, \qquad \omega_2=\omega_3-\omega_1. \]

The nonlinear polarizations are

\[ P_1 = 4\varepsilon_0d_{\mathrm{eff}} A_3A_2^*e^{i(k_3-k_2)z}, \]
\[ P_2 = 4\varepsilon_0d_{\mathrm{eff}} A_3A_1^*e^{i(k_3-k_1)z}. \]

Therefore,

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial A_1}{\partial z} &= i\frac{2d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_1^2}{k_1c^2} A_3(z)A_2^*(z)e^{-i\Delta kz}, \\[8pt] \frac{\partial A_2}{\partial z} &= i\frac{2d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_2^2}{k_2c^2} A_3(z)A_1^*(z)e^{-i\Delta kz}, \\[8pt] \frac{\partial A_3}{\partial z} &= i\frac{2d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_3^2}{k_3c^2} A_1(z)A_2(z)e^{i\Delta kz}. \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

Phase (Mis)match Considerations#

Undepleted-pump approximation#

We assume that \(A_3\) remains small compared with \(A_1\) and \(A_2\). Therefore, \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) are taken to be independent of \(z\):

\[ \frac{\partial A_3}{\partial z} = i\frac{2d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_3^2}{k_3c^2} A_1A_2e^{i\Delta kz}. \]

Perfect phase matching: \(\Delta k=0\)#

To make things even simpler, assume perfect phase matching:

\[ \Delta k=0. \]

Then \(A_3(z)\) increases linearly with \(z\). Therefore, the intensity \(I_3(z)\) increases quadratically:

\[ A_3(z)\propto z, \qquad I_3(z)\propto z^2. \]

Phase mismatch: \(\Delta k\neq0\)#

Now consider

\[ \Delta k\neq0. \]

Integrating from \(z=0\) to \(z=L\),

\[ A_3(L) = i\frac{2d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_3^2}{k_3c^2} A_1A_2 \int_0^L e^{i\Delta kz}\,dz, \]

so that

\[ A_3(L) = i\frac{2d_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_3^2}{k_3c^2} A_1A_2 \frac{e^{i\Delta kL}-1}{i\Delta k}. \]

Intensity of the generated wave#

To see the effect of \(\Delta k\neq0\), calculate \(I_3\):

\[ I_3 = \frac{cn_3\varepsilon_0}{2}|A_3|^2 = \frac{2\varepsilon_0d_{\mathrm{eff}}^2\omega_3^2}{n_3c^2} |A_1|^2|A_2|^2 \left| \frac{e^{i\Delta kL}-1}{\Delta k} \right|^2. \]

Also,

\[ c\varepsilon_0=\frac{1}{377\ \Omega}. \]

Sinc dependence#

Using

\[ \left| \frac{e^{i\Delta kL}-1}{\Delta k} \right|^2 = L^2 \operatorname{sinc}^2 \left(\frac{\Delta kL}{2}\right), \]

the phase-mismatch dependence is expressed by the familiar \(\operatorname{sinc}^2\) function.

Expressing \(I_3\) in terms of \(I_1\) and \(I_2\)#

Using

\[ I_1=\frac{cn_1\varepsilon_0}{2}|A_1|^2, \qquad I_2=\frac{cn_2\varepsilon_0}{2}|A_2|^2, \]

we obtain

\[ I_3 = 8 \frac{ d_{\mathrm{eff}}^2\omega_3^2 I_1 I_2 }{ n_1n_2n_3\varepsilon_0c^3 } L^2 \operatorname{sinc}^2 \left( \frac{\Delta kL}{2} \right). \]

Following the supplied notes,

\[ L\equiv L_{\mathrm{coh}}=\frac{2}{\Delta k}. \]
